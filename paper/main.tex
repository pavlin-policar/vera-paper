%% Abstract section.
\abstract{%
Two-dimensional embeddings resulting from dimensionality reduction techniques, such as t-SNE, UMAP, and multidimensional scaling, are widely used in various scientific fields to visualize high-dimensional data. These visualizations provide a useful tool for exploratory data analysis, allowing researchers to identify clusters, outliers, and other interesting patterns in the data. However, interpreting the resulting visualizations can be challenging, as it often requires additional analysis to understand the differences between data points in different regions of the embedding space. To address this issue, we propose VECA (Visual Explanations and Contrastive Analysis), an automatic embedding-annotation approach that generates visual explanations for any two-dimensional embedding. Motivated by insights from social science, VECA is designed to produce informative and contrastive explanations that best characterize distinct regions in the visualization compared to the remainder of the embedding space. Unlike existing approaches, VECA automatically performs both the identification and selection of possible visual explanations according to their information content and contrastive importance, then generates a small but diverse set of informative explanations. We demonstrate the utility of VECA on several high-dimensional datasets, including transcriptomics data.

Our open-source, Python implementation of VECA is freely-available at \url{https://github.com/pavlin-policar/veca}.
}

\firstsection{Introduction}

\maketitle

Two-dimensional, point-based visualizations of high-dimensional data are ubiquitous throughout various domains of science, including biology, economics, chemistry, political science, astronomy, and physics~\cite{Liu2017}.
With the boom of big data, analyzing data sets with tens of thousands of features and millions of rows has become commonplace.
Data science is today equipped with a plethora of methods for dimensionality reduction, including principal components analysis (PCA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), FreeViz~\cite{Demsar2007}, multidimensional scaling (MDS)~\cite{Kruskal1978}, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)~\cite{Maaten2008}, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)~\cite{McInnes2018}.
We can use these to uncover underlying data structures, such as clusters, outliers, or transitions, and to support exploratory data analysis and model interpretation.

Linear methods, such as PCA, LDA, and FreeViz, have intuitive explanations of their inferred dimensions. However, they are limited in the types of high-dimensional structures they can uncover. In contrast, non-linear dimensionality approaches, such as MDS, t-SNE, and UMAP, can reveal complex structures and patterns in the underlying data manifold. However, untangling any meaning from their depictions becomes impossible. With the development of ever more scalable dimensionality reduction techniques, one typically generates several embeddings during exploratory data analysis using different parameter settings and algorithms, each revealing various aspects of the data.

Although many techniques are available to identify distortions in non-linear embeddings~\cite{Nonato2018}, there is limited research on explaining these embeddings. Understanding the structures that emerge from non-linear embeddings can be highly beneficial in practical applications, as it can accelerate hypothesis generation and aid in quickly identifying data collection errors. Current methods for comprehending non-linear embeddings involve post-processing techniques or interactive tools to scrutinize each embedding. However, this process can be laborious and time-consuming, frequently necessitating an extensive examination of each embedding. Two-dimensional embeddings can also aid in interpreting deep-learning models, where visualizations are typically generated from the learned latent embeddings. Examining how these latent embeddings correspond to the original input variables can shed light on the inner workings of these deep models. Several approaches have focused on developing novel techniques for visualizing and explaining embeddings in terms of their original input variables. However, these are often limited to a single explanation~\cite{DaSilva2015,VanDriel2020,Tian2021} or require the user to manually inspect all possible input variables~\cite{Faust2018,Sohns2022}.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inspired by insights from social sciences~\cite{Miller2019}, we propose a conceptual framework for generating and selecting visual explanations.
  \item Building on our conceptual framework, we develop VECA (Visual Explanations and Contrastive Analysis), an automatic, end-to-end, visual explanation approach for explaining any two-dimensional embeddings.
  \item We demonstrate the utility of VECA on three real-world data sets and show its utility in support of understanding feature localization in the embedding space.
  \item We demonstrate how to use VECA in the context of deep learning, showcasing its role in understanding model predictions and errors. We illustrate its use in generating hypotheses for the types of relationships these opaque deep models may have learned.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Although a significant amount of research has been dedicated to identifying distortions in the generated embeddings of multi-dimensional or unstructured data, explaining these embeddings has received relatively little attention. Nonato and Aupetit~\cite{Nonato2018} provide an excellent overview of embedding distortions and their associated methods. Building on research from the social sciences, Miller~\cite{Miller2019} argues that three cognitive processes are involved in explanation: explanation generation, selection, and evaluation. We contend that most existing work has focused primarily on the first process—explanation generation—resulting in the development of methods that aim to understand specific aspects of embeddings.

For instance, Sohns et al.~\cite{Sohns2022} develop rangesets, a visualization technique to help the user visualize regions in the embedding where particular value ranges of a specific variable occur.
Faust et al.~\cite{Faust2018} develop DimReader, a perturbation-based approach that generates contour maps corresponding to a given input variable in the embedding space. Both techniques require the user to inspect plots corresponding to each input variable and manually discover which input features are important.

Cheng and Mueller~\cite{Cheng2015} designed Data Context Map, an early approach aimed at explaining two-dimensional embeddings. They augment MDS by embedding data points corresponding to various input variables into the same embedding space. However, this approach is prohibitively slow for all but the smallest data sets and applies only to embeddings created by the MDS. Fujiwara et al.~\cite{Fujiwara2019} propose ccPCA, a contrastive learning-based algorithm that reveals dimensions contributing to forming a particular cluster. ppPCA is limited to the PCA dimensionality reduction method, which has its own set of limitations.

Da Silva et al.~\cite{DaSilva2015} develop the first approach suitable for explaining any two-dimensional embeddings. They aim to identify the most informative input variable for each data point in the embedding. Their approach was later extended by Van Driel et al.~\cite{VanDriel2020} and Tian et al.~\cite{Tian2021}. In their work, each point is explained by only a single input variable, often obscuring other potentially informative characteristics of the embedding space. Additionally, their method produces only one explanation, potentially missing alternative explanations usually available for data sets containing many variables.

Several interactive tools have been proposed to facilitate the explanation of individual embeddings~\cite{Kandogan2012,Heulot2013,Martins2014,Marcilio2021,Li2023}. While involving users in the process can be helpful, it can also introduce biases when enumerating alternative explanations and impose a significant burden on users when exploring a large space of explanation variants. Manual exploration can also limit the number of different embeddings that a user is willing to examine. With the advent of more scalable approaches for generating two-dimensional embeddings, it may be advantageous to generate multiple embeddings using different algorithms and parameter values, as each may highlight different aspects of the data.

\section{Methods}

In the previous section, we laid out the conceptual framework for visual explanations. Here, we describe \textit{Visual Explanations and Contrastive Analysis (VECA)}, our fully-automated approach for generating visual explanations for any two-dimensional embeddings.

\subsection{Region construction}

Our region construction approach uses kernel density estimate computed on each explanatory variable. First, we generate all potentially informative explanatory variables from the input variables. These can correspond to specific values, sets of values, or ranges of values. Next, using these explanatory variables, we generate explanatory regions, represented by collections of polygons, which correspond to the spatial areas in the embedding space enriched with a particular explanatory variable. Our approach can produce over-fragmented regions, so we develop a post-hoc merging procedure that reduces the number of possible explanatory regions and improves visual coherence by merging highly-overlapping regions. Below, we in detail describe each of these steps.

\subsubsection{Extracting explanatory variables}

The input data set can contain both discrete and continuous variables. Since we want to explain the embedding in terms of the input variables' different values or ranges of values, we expand each input variable into a collection of explanatory variables. We use $k$-Means quantization for continuous variables to split the values into $k$ discrete explanatory variables. By default, we split into 5 bins, corresponding to the typical five-point scale: ``very low'', ``low'', ``medium'', ``high'', and ``very high''. Discrete variables are one-hot encoded, resulting in $k$ different explanatory indicator variables, each corresponding to a particular categorical value of the input variable.

\subsubsection{Extracting explanatory regions}
\label{sec:kde_estimation}

Having extracted our explanatory variables, we next determine the regions with enrichment of the values of these explanatory variables.

The first step in our approach is to identify the scale of the embedding. Different embedding algorithms produce embeddings that can span across different scales, e.g., one embedding may span from $-5$ to $5$ along its dimensions, while another may span from $-50$ to $50$. Additionally, using a single scale may not make sense for data sets containing different numbers of samples. For instance, in a dataset with 50 samples, we are likely to be interested in groups of 10-20 points, while in a dataset containing 2 million samples, these small groups will be less interesting and could result from noise. To determine the scale $\sigma$ of each embedding, we first determine the scale of the groups we are interested in. We select $k=\sqrt{N}$, commonly used in nearest neighbors classification. We then compute the median distance of each point's $k$-th nearest neighbor and use that as the embedding scale.

Next, we obtain each explanatory variable's kernel density estimate $D$ using a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth $\sigma$. Next, we extract the contours of the density estimate at a user-defined level, typically at $0.25 \cdot \max D$. Note that because the density estimate may be multimodal, e.g., if an explanatory variable is enriched in different regions of the space, we may obtain more than one contour. These contours represent the regions we use in subsequent steps of our analysis and correspond to the spatial areas of the space in which each particular explanatory variable is enriched.

\subsubsection{Post-hoc explanatory variable merging}
\label{sec:merge_overfragmented}

It is unlikely that five bins will result in the optimal discretization of any particular continuous variable. It is also unlikely that all categories associated with a particular categorical variable contain entirely unrelated concepts.
The resulting explanatory variables produced by discretization and one-hot-encoding are often overly fragmented, resulting in difficult-to-understand and cluttered visualizations. To remedy this, we apply a post-hoc merging procedure to explanatory variables originating from the same input variable. 

For ordinal and continuous variables, we only allow merging consecutive bins. For instance, the discretization $\{ x < 5, 5 \leq x < 6, 6 \geq x\}$ for a continuous variable could be merged into $\{x < 6, 6 \geq x\}$, but not into $\{ 5 \leq x < 6, x < 5 \mid x \geq 6\}$.
The same reasoning applies to ordinal categorical variables, merging only adjacent categories. For instance, the ordinal values $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ could be merged into $\{\{1, 2, 3\}, \{4\}\}$, but not into $\{\{1, 4\}, \{2, 3\}\}$.
We apply no such restriction to nominal categorical variables. E.g., the categorical variable values $\{red, green, blue\}$ could be merged into $\{\{red, green\}, \{blue\}\}$.

Our merging procedure accounts for the spatial location of the regions embedding, and explanatory variables are merged when their corresponding regions exhibit a high degree of overlap.
More concretely, to merge two explanatory variables, we require that their sample overlap is at least 50\%. 
\begin{equation}
\text{overlap}(r_1, r_2) = |r_1 \cap r_2| \notag
\end{equation}
where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the regions in question, and $|r_1 \cap r_2|$ denotes the cardinality of their intersection.
We also require that the resulting merged region improves the overall purity of the regions by at least 5\%. We define the purity of a region as
\begin{equation}
\text{purity}(r) = \frac{1}{|r|} \sum_{i \in r} x_i \notag
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the region of interest, $|r|$ is the number of points within the region, and $x_i$ is the binary explanatory variable value.
We also require that the merged region does not decrease the spatial localization is not decreased. We measure spatial localization using Geary's C spatial autocorrelation index, defined as
\begin{equation}
C=\frac{N-1}{2 \sum_{ij} W } \frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{i j}\left(x_i-x_j\right)^2}{\sum_i\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2},\notag
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of data samples, $W$ is a weighted adjacency matrix indicating the spatial proximities between data points, and $x_i$ is the feature value of sample $i$.
We compute the adjacency matrix $W$ by finding all nearest neighbors at a distance of at most $3 \cdot \sigma$ and evaluate a Gaussian probability density function centered on each point using the same scale $\sigma$ from Sec.~\ref{sec:kde_estimation}.

Having merged our regions results in new, larger regions, these may now also overlap sufficiently to be merged. Therefore we perform this merging procedure iteratively until no further merges can be performed. Fig.~\ref{fig:overfragmentation}) shows an example of over-fragmentation and the effect of subsequent merging.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{discretization-merging-1}
  \caption{(a) shows the results of applying discretization to the \textit{Hillshade 3pm}. We plot a histogram showing the distribution of variable values and mark bin edges with vertical red lines. The corresponding scatter plot shows the embedding colored according to each point's corresponding bin. As the scatter plot shows, splitting this variable into five bins leads to over-fragmentation resulting in no clear separation between samples of different colors. We apply our post-hoc merging approach to reduce over-fragmentation in the embedding space. We plot the results in (b), where the bins more naturally correspond to spatial regions in the embedding space, creating a clear separation between low and high attribute values in the scatter plot.}
  \label{fig:overfragmentation}
\end{figure}

If no particular variable value is localized to a specific region of the embedding space, our merging procedure could merge all the explanatory variables for a variable, leaving a single region encompassing the entire space. This result is desirable, as it indicates that this particular variable holds no bearing when explaining the embedding; we discard such variables from future consideration for generating visual explanations.

\subsection{Contrastive explanations}

Contrastive explanations of features aim to show where different feature values occur in the embedding.

\subsubsection{Explanation construction}

Generating contrastive explanations is straightforward: we include all its derived explanatory variables for each input variable. While the different regions may overlap somewhat, our explanatory variable merging procedure ensures that regions with significant overlap are merged beforehand.

\subsubsection{Explanation selection}

The construction procedure provides a collection of possible contrastive explanations, one for each input variable. However, some explanations may be more informative than others.
Intuitively, a variable whose values are localized in disjoint regions of the space will be the most informative and more appropriate to reason about than a variable whose values are distributed more evenly.

To adhere to the principle of ``simple explanations'', we aim to present the user with as few plots as possible. We achieve this by trying to merge regions with sufficient overlap and bipartite mapping between pairs of variables where the edges in the graph are created when there is sufficient overlap between the two regions. By default, we require an overlap of at least 95\% of samples in both regions. This can also be used as a visual proxy for identifying feature correlations, sometimes even non-linear ones.

As plots containing larger numbers of explanatory variables tend to be more difficult to understand, we prefer visual explanations containing a smaller number of explanatory variables, {\em e.g.}, between 2-5. To achieve this, we impose a Gaussian prior on the number of contained explanatory variables with $\mu=3$ and $\sigma^2=2$.


\subsection{Descriptive Explanations}

With descriptive explanations, we aim to show interesting combinations of explanatory variables such that the resulting plots are as informative as possible. These visualizations can include interesting combinations of input variables, leading us to uncover interesting relationships between specific variable values.

\subsubsection{Explanation construction}

In descriptive explanations, we can no longer show explanatory variables from a single input variable. Still, we can choose any combination of explanatory variables from any input variable.
Commonly, several explanatory variables appear in similar regions of space. For instance, if Fig.~\ref{fig:bipartite_matching}, the three input variables \textit{petal length}, \textit{petal width}, and \textit{species} are highly correlated and occupy roughly the same regions of the space. We merge these into a single visual explanation to reduce the number of possible visual explanations. We only do this when sufficient overlap exists between all pairs of regions. By default, we require that at least 90\% of the encompassed samples are shared between any two pairs of merged regions. Note that the samples need not necessarily have that particular feature value; it only needs to be encompassed by the region.
We also require that the resulting merged cluster has a purity of at least 50\%, i.e., at least 50\% of all samples encompassed within this new region have at least one of the merged variables.
As an additional requirement, we require that each resulting cluster has a Geary's C index of, at most, $0.5$. Note that this step differs from the merging procedure described in Sec.~\ref{sec:merge_overfragmented}, as we do not limit ourselves to merging adjacent bins or explanatory variables derived from the same input variable.

\begin{figure}[thb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{iris-bipartite-matching}
  \caption{The plots in panels (a), (b), and (c) correspond to contrastive explanations for three different variables in the Iris data set. The variables represent \textit{petal length}, \textit{petal width}, and \textit{iris species}, respectively. Notice that each of these three plots contains regions that occupy the same regions of the space. For instance, we would lose almost no information if the regions corresponding to \textit{petal length < 2.46}, \textit{petal width < 0.67}, and \textit{species = `iris setosa'} were merged into a single region. A similar mapping can be found between the other two subregions in each of the three variables. VECA merges these types of contrastive explanations wherever possible into a single one, presenting the user with a smaller number of visual explanations, easing the cognitive burden on the end-user.}
  \label{fig:bipartite_matching}
\end{figure}

Having merged overlapping explanatory variables, we next need to find combinations of these resulting regions so that they produce informative visual explanations.
To avoid cluttering the plot, we want to include variables in each visual explanation so that we have as little overlap as possible. We achieve this by casting this as a graph-independent set problem. We first compute the overlap graph between all explanatory variables, placing an edge between explanatory variables if the overlap between regions exceeds the maximum user-specified threshold, by default 20\% of overlapping samples. Then, the independent sets of this graph are found from the combinations of non-overlapping explanatory variables which do not exceed this prespecified threshold.

\subsubsection{Explanation selection}

Unlike contrastive explanations, descriptive explanations are not limited to including only explanatory regions for a single variable. We instead want to find a combination of explanatory regions, perhaps from different variables, that are as informative as possible. What "informative as possible" means will be defined later on.

Once we have generated the explanatory regions for the relevant variables, we need to rank them according to their importance and informativeness. To do this, we consider several factors.
Firstly, we prioritize simple explanations over complex ones. We defined the term ``simplicity'' to mean that it is easily understandable by any end user. Practically, this means preferring explanations containing two to five minimally-overlapping explanatory variables which are highly localized in distinct regions of the space. Additionally, we prefer explanations that cover as much of the embedding as possible.

We achieve this by calculating a score for each embedding layout by considering the following factors. To minimize overlap between regions, we compute the sample overlap between all pairs of regions. To prefer simpler, non-fragmented regions, we calculate the number of polygons each explanatory region contains and calculate the overall polygon-to-region ratio for each visual explanation. We prefer explanations where this ratio is closer to one, as this indicates that each explanatory variable has one associated polygon. To maximize embedding coverage, we compute the percentage of total points in the embedding contained within any single region in the visual explanation. To prefer explanations with a smaller number of explanatory variables, {\em e.g.}, between 2-5, we impose a Gaussian prior with $\mu=3$ and $\sigma^2=2$. We then evaluate the probability density function at the number of contained variables in the visual explanations. Intuitively, a higher value will be assigned when the visual explanation contains three explanatory variables than when it contains two explanatory variables.

Another factor to consider is explanation diversity. Unlike in contrastive explanations, where each subsequent explanation is completely different from one another, and the user is exposed to completely different explanatory variables with each visual explanation, visual explanations can be repeated as often as possible in descriptive explanations. However, intuitively, if a user has seen a particular explanatory variable in the first plot, it would be more informative for the next several visual explanations to not contain this explanatory variable and prefer explanatory variables that the user has not yet seen.

To achieve this diversity in visual explanations, we develop an iterative visual explanation selection technique. In addition to the layout scores, which can be computed independently for each layout separately, we introduce an explanatory variable penalty which is applied to each visual explanation. Importantly, this penalty depends on the previously selected visual explanations, and consecutive visual explanations which contain repeating visual explanations are heavily penalized. As we don't want explanatory variables to be shown only once, we want them not to appear in two or three plots in a row. We apply an exponential decay to this variable penalty, so a variable is no longer penalized after {\em e.g.} three visual explanations. This approach enforces diversity in the produced visual explanations and prevents explanatory variables from being repeated too frequently in the visual explanations. Thus, the user can better understand different regions of different explanatory variables while looking at a small number of visual explanations.

\section{Case studies}

In the following, we present three case studies of increasing complexity. We begin by running through our analysis on the simple Iris flowers data set~\cite{Anderson1936}, which is well-known and easy to understand. We continue with the Wine Quality data set~\cite{Cortez2009}, which contains more input variables, and requires more explanation. We conclude by applying our approach on the large Forest Cover Type data set~\cite{Blackard1999}, containing over half a million data points, and demonstrate how VECA can be used to gain insight into what kinds of errors predictive models may make and also to understand what kinds of relationships between variables deep-learning models may be learning.

We use default parameter settings in the VECA Python library unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Iris Flowers}

We walk through the different steps of our analysis using the Iris data set from Anderson~\cite{Anderson1936} as a guiding example. This small data set profiles 150 iris flowers with four continuous variables containing physical measurements of individual flowers (sepal length, sepal width, petal length, petal width) and their corresponding species ({\em Iris setosa}, {\em Iris versicolor}, {\em Iris virginica}).

We first construct a two-dimensional t-SNE embedding of the data set with parameter values metric=cosine, perplexity=30 using the \textsf{openTSNE} library~\cite{Policar2019}. As is common practice with predictive tasks, we do not use the \textit{Species} variable in the embedding construction. We then explain the resulting embedding using VECA. For this example only, we perform discretization into three bins instead of five, as this better demonstrates the types of things one must take into consideration when constructing visual explanations. The different steps involved in the pipeline are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{iris_pipeline.pdf}
  \caption{We illustrate the region construction process on the Iris data set. We omit the \textit{petal width} and \textit{species} variables for brevity.}
  \label{fig:iris_pipeline}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.a shows the standard approach to explaining embeddings in terms of their original features. The points in the scatter plot are colored according to their feature values. This approach works well for a small number of input features, but quickly grows unwieldy as the number of input features increases. Additionally, the plots are typically shown in no particular order, necessitating the inspection of every single input variable. Finally, colors correspond to different feature values, and their usefulness is determined by the color palette used. In general, it is fairly easy to distinguish between small and large values of the feature. For instance, the top panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.a shows a clear distinction between feature values on the plot's left-hand and right-hand sides of the plot.
However, suppose the variable distribution is multimodal, as it appears in the middle panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.b. In that case, it is generally difficult to tell these apart from one another, and the colors begin to blend.

Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.b shows each input variable's initial derived explanatory variables. Notice that the middle and bottom panels contain regions with a high degree of overlap. For instance, in the middle panel, the orange region corresponding to the explanatory variable \textit{sepal length (cm) > 6.55} overlaps almost entirely with the green region corresponding to \textit{5.53 < sepal length (cm) < 6.55}. These two discretizations could be merged into a single interval \textit{sepal length (cm) > 5.53} at minimal loss of information and would declutter the visualization. Indeed, this is exactly what happens in the next step of the VECA pipeline. The third row of Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline} shows the result of our post-hoc merging procedure.

The bottom panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.b demonstrates a more complex scenario. The blue region corresponding to \textit{sepal width (cm) < 2.85} is localized to the right cluster, and the green region corresponding to \textit{sepal width (cm) > 3.44} is localized to the left cluster. However, the intermediate values corresponding to  \textit{2.85 < sepal width (cm) < 3.44} in the orange region are present throughout the embedding.
We may consider merging the orange region with the green region, producing an explanatory variable corresponding to \textit{sepal width (cm) > 2.85}; however, this may mislead users to believe there are only two groups of feature values, with high values distributed evenly throughout the entire embedding, and low values appearing only in the right-hand cluster.
Alternatively, we may instead choose to merge the \textit{sepal width (cm) < 2.85} and \textit{2.85 < sepal width (cm) < 3.44} regions, but this may lead to a similarly misleading visualization.
Alternatively, we may choose to leave the regions as they are, but this would result in a highly cluttered visualization.
In VECA, we merge all three clusters into a single explanatory variable. The resulting explanatory variable contains a single region encompassing the entirety of the embedding space. This is not particularly informative and is discarded from any downstream explanations. Other approaches may be possible, but removing unclear or cluttered visual explanations is better than providing misleading or complex explanations.

Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.c shows the explanatory variables after our post-hoc merging procedure. Notice that the first panel remains the same as in Fig.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.b, as the initially proposed regions have a low degree of overlap. The explanatory variables in the middle panel were merged into two explanatory variables, while the explanatory variables in the bottom panel were merged into one, as previously discussed.
Note that the results of our merging procedure depend on the number of selected bins used during discretization. For instance, had we chosen five bins, as is the default in VECA, the orange region in the bottom panel may have been further split into a left and right region, and the resulting merged region may have been similar to the one shown in the middle panel.

Figs.~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.d and ~\ref{fig:iris_pipeline}.e show the generated contrastive and descriptive explanations for this embedding. Due to the small number of variables and their high degree of correlation, there are only two visual explanations for each explanation type. Notice that the first panel in the contrastive explanations displays three variables. This is possible as the three variables all contain regions that occupy the same regions of the space. The visual explanations indicate that the classification of Iris flowers is fairly straightforward. One would only need to consider \textit{petal width} and \textit{petal length} to obtain high accuracy. As this data set is fairly straightforward, the descriptive explanations reveal nothing that the contrastive explanations don't.

\subsection{Wine Quality}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{wine_explanations.pdf}
  \caption{We apply our approach to the wine quality data set. We plot the top four contrastive explanations in panels a-d and the top four descriptive explanations in panels e-h. The contrastive explanations each show where different values of a single input variable appear in the embedding. The descriptive explanations depicted in panels e-h aim to show maximally-informative combinations of the explanatory variables.}
  \label{fig:wine_explanations}
\end{figure*}

The wine quality data set~\cite{Cortez2009} contains 6,497 samples of two common red and white wine variants from a popular winery in the Minho region in Portugal. Each sample is described by 12 physicochemical features, including fixed acidity, residual sugar, and alcohol. The data set is typically used in wine-quality prediction. As before, we generate a two-dimensional t-SNE embedding of the data set using parameter values metric=cosine, perplexity=30, exaggeration=2, excluding the target variable from the embedding construction. We then explain the resulting embedding using VECA using the original input variables. The top four contrastive explanations and the top four descriptive explanations are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.

Figs.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.a and \ref{fig:wine_explanations}.b reveal that different values of the \textit{Wine Type} and \textit{Chlorides} variables appear in distinct regions of the embedding, while the variables \textit{Total Sulfur Oxide} and \textit{Residual sugar} in Figs.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.c and \ref{fig:wine_explanations}.d exhibit a small degree of overlap. Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.a indicates that the two types of wines are clearly separated from one another in the embedding. This variable was not used to construct the embedding, indicating substantial differences between the underlying chemical properties of red and white wines. However, we identify a small cluster between the two large groups of wine that is only partially included in the orange region, and its neighboring samples do not appear to belong to either red or white wine. We inspect the underlying samples for each wine type in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_separation}. The plot reveals that most white and red wines are indeed contained within the two regions, with minimal mixing between red and white wines. The plot also shows that this in-between cluster is somewhat mixed, indicating a small group of red and white wines with similar chemical properties.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wine_type_separation.pdf}
  \caption{We plot each region from Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}. (a) highlights the points corresponding to red wine, while (b) highlights the points corresponding to white wine. We see that there is minimal mixing between the two wine types. We also observe that the small in-between cluster contains a rough mix of 50\% white wines and 50\% red wines.}
  \label{fig:wine_separation}
\end{figure}

Descriptive visual explanations can also lead to the discovery of correlations between explanatory variables. For instance, the blue region on the left-hand side of Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.e is comprised of three explanatory variables: \textit{wine type = 'red'}, \textit{total sulfur dioxide < 60.13}, and \textit{0.06 < chlorides < 0.13}. We can thus conclude that red wines generally contain lower levels of \textit{total sulfur dioxide} than white wines and that their levels of \textit{chlorides} generally fall between 0.06 and 0.13. We can consult the contrastive explanations to determine whether these levels of \textit{chlorides} are high or low. Inspecting Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_separation}.b, we can conclude that this is a middle-range value of chlorides.

We next demonstrate that discretization alone can be helpful in the visualization of feature values, especially in the presence of outlier values.
A common approach for explaining embeddings in terms of a particular input feature is to color-code each point in the scatter plot based on its feature value. However, this approach is sensitive to outliers that distort the color scale, resulting in a highly skewed point-color distribution.
For instance, Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.a shows the distribution of the values of the \textit{Residual Sugar} variable. Notice that the histogram is skewed to the left due to a small number of extremely high values. Consequently, the resulting scatterplot does not provide much useful information, as the points appear more-or-less colored similarly. A common remedy to this problem is to log-transform the values. However, this is not always effective and increases the cognitive load on the analyst, as now they must think about variable values in terms of their log scale. Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.b shows the log-transformed values of the original \textit{Residual Sugar} input variable.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wine_discretization.pdf}
  \caption{We compare our approach to the typical approach to explaining embedding with the original input variables using scatterplot color coding. We use the \textit{Residual Sugar} variable from the wine quality data set~\cite{Cortez2009} as an example. (a) shows a typical color-coding scheme, where the color scale is skewed as a result of a few outliers. Consequently, the plot is not informative. We apply a log transform to fix this issue in (b), but this now requires reasoning about the values in the log-scale. (c) show our discretization procedure without any data transformations. Due to discretization, the visualization does not suffer from this problem, while the final discretization in (d) additionally merges variable values into three bins corresponding to `low', `medium', and `high` concentrations of `Residual Sugar'.}
  \label{fig:wine_discretization}
\end{figure}

Applying our discretization approach mitigates this problem. Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.c shows that discretization limits the effects of outliers. These often get put into one bin and have a minimal negative effect on the colors in the resulting scatterplot. Notice that the scatterplot in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.c looks similar to that in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.b. However, we can still reason about the variable values in terms of their original scales. Applying our merging procedure merges spatially overlapping bins, resulting in a cleaner overall visualization. The scatter plot in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_discretization}.d shows three distinct bins, where the right-hand side of the scatter plot seems to be characterized by higher values of \textit{Residual Sugar}, which transition to lower values as we move to the left-hand side. Notice that the outlier values were all correctly bundled with the higher values.

\subsection{Forest Cover Type}

We here adopt a similar evaluation strategy as Sohns et al.~\cite{Sohns2022}, as it nicely illustrates how visual explanations can be used to aid in the interpretation of deep models and uncover the inferred relationships between the input and target variables.

The forest cover type data set~\cite{Blackard1999} contains 581,012 samples described by cartographic variables, including elevation, aspect, and distance to roadways. The dataset is primarily used in supervised learning settings, where the aim is to distinguish between seven forest cover types.
As in the previous two examples, we generate a two-dimensional t-SNE embedding of the full data set using parameter values metric=cosine, perplexity=500, and exaggeration=4. We then explain the resulting embedding using VECA in terms of the original data features and show the top four contrastive explanations in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions}.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{covertype_contrastive.pdf}
  \caption{Forest cover type contrastive explanations}
  \label{fig:covertype_explantions}
\end{figure}

The contrastive layouts in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions} reveal that \textit{Wilderness Area}, i.e., the national park where the samples are located, is the primary variable driving cluster formation in the embedding space. This indicates that the composition of trees in different areas of a particular national park is more similar than the composition of trees between areas in different national parks. This may be undesirable if, for instance, our ultimate goal is to apply our model to other national parks.

As the goal with this data set is the prediction of the forest cover type, it is encouraging to see that the \textit{Cover Type} variable appears to be somewhat localized in the embedding space, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions}.d. The plot reveals two distinct groups of forest cover types, the orange group corresponding to \textit{Cottonwood/Willow}, \textit{Douglas-Fir}, and \textit{Ponderosa Pine} cover types and the blue group to the remaining four cover types. This localization indicates that machine learning models will likely have little difficulty separating samples between these two groups of cover types. For instance, Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions}.b suggests that we could fairly separate the orange cover types from the blue cover types based only on the \textit{Elevation} variable alone.
While Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions}.d indicates mixing between the forest cover types contained within the orange and blue regions, this does not necessarily mean machine learning models will struggle to differentiate between the different cover types.
The embedding in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions} was constructed in an unsupervised way using the t-SNE algorithm, which positions similar samples close to one another. In this case, the similarities between data points were computed using all available features. However, in a supervised setting, the model may determine that some input variables do not relate to the prediction task and may ignore them altogether. Omitting these features from the embedding construction process would produce a different embedding, which may better separate the different forest cover types.

To illustrate this point, we fit a four-layer neural network with two hidden layers (dimensions 256 and 128) using ReLU activation units. We use the same data split as in the original publication~\cite{Blackard1999}. We optimize the model using the Adam optimizer for 2,000 epochs. Our final model achieves $\sim50\%$ predictive accuracy, lower than the $\sim70\%$ reported by Blackard and Dean~\cite{Blackard1999}.
We note that our goal here is not to fit the most precise predictive model but to demonstrate how VECA can aid in model debugging and interpretation.
To better understand what our trained deep-learning model is doing, we extract the latent embeddings of all the data points and generate a two-dimensional t-SNE embedding using the same parameters as before. We then use VECA to explain this embedding regarding the original data features.

Ideally, the two-dimensional embedding would reveal seven clusters, each corresponding to a distinct forest cover type. This would indicate a perfect separation between the different classes. However, achieving this level of separation in most real-world data sets is rare. Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.a shows the resulting t-SNE embedding. The plot depicts an elongated structure containing all the data points with no clear clusters. However, the visual explanation corresponding to the \textit{Cover Type} variable in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.f shows that the model could not establish clear boundaries between the different forest cover types, it has learned to differentiate between some groups of cover types. For instance, the orange region corresponding to Cottonwood/Willows, Douglas Firs, and Ponderosa Pines is located on the opposite side of the embedding than the green region associated with the Krummholz cover type. This indicates that our model should be able to distinguish samples between these two classes. On the other hand, the overlap between the green and red regions associated with Krummholz and Spruce/Fir cover types suggests that the model may have difficulty differentiating between these categories. The confusion matrix in Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.b confirms both of these hypotheses: our model can distinguish between Krummholz samples and Cottonwood/Willows, Douglas Firs, and Ponderosa Pines samples with perfect accuracy but misclassifies some samples with Krummholz cover type as Spruce/Fir cover types.

\begin{figure}[thb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{covertype_model.pdf}
  \caption{Forest cover type model. The abbreviated class names in the confusion matrix correspond to Cottonwood/Willow (CW), Douglas-Fir (DF), Ponderosa Pine (PP), Aspen (AS), Lodgepole Pine (LP), Spruce/Fir (SF), and Krummholz (KR).}
  \label{fig:covertype_model}
\end{figure}

VECA can also be used to aid in model interpretation and provide hints into the types of variable relationships that deep-learning models may be inferring. While it is possible to directly inspect the model's weights and determine the feature relationships and importance through specialized explanatory techniques, visual explanations offer an alternative way to gain a sense of what is happening under the hood. We generate contrastive layouts to the t-SNE plot of the latent embeddings and show the top-four layouts in Figs.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.c-f.

The contrastive visual explanations in Figs.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.c, \ref{fig:covertype_model}.d, and \ref{fig:covertype_model}.f suggest that the model may have learned a relationship between specific soil types, high elevations, and the Krummholz forest cover type. Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.e shows that the wilderness area variable, i.e., the geographic location from which each sample was collected, also explains some aspects of the latent embedding. This spatial correlation could indicate overfitting if, for instance, our goal was to develop a model that could classify forest cover type regardless of geographic location. Conversely, if our goal was to classify only samples from these four geographic areas, relying on the geographic location may be a desirable property as forest cover types are likely to differ between national parks.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_explantions}, VECA identified the \textit{Hillshade 3pm} variable as one of the most informative contrastive variables for explaining that particular embedding. However, its absence from Fig.~\ref{fig:covertype_model} suggests this variable was not as important for the prediction of forest cover type. On the other hand, the \textit{Soil Type} variable appears at least somewhat useful for the prediction task, while it was not as important for explaining the original embedding.

We warn that these visual explanations should not be viewed as definitive, and one should only use them to generate hypotheses that require further investigation. When embedding a high-dimensional data set into two dimensions, distortions will inevitably be introduced in the resulting visualization, and we should view the relationships between data points should subsequently with a healthy degree of skepticism. However, a spatial correlation in the embedding space suggests that the model has assigned some value to that particular feature and has learned some relationship between the input variable and the target class. Otherwise, the variable would likely be fairly decorrelated with the embedding space. In summary, visual explanations can serve as a good starting point in interpreting deep models, but additional analysis and validation are necessary to confirm any initial hypotheses.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}

The region construction step of approach could be replaced by alpha-hulls from Sohns et al.~\cite{Sohns2022} or any other one of the embedding enrichment schemes outlined by Nonato and Aupetit~\cite{Nonato2018}. Alpha-hulls may even benefit in terms of purity. However, this could be problematic if the users chooses to insert new data points into existing embeddings. Modern implementations of t-SNE~\cite{Policar2019} and UMAP~\cite{McInnes2018} both enable users to insert out-of-sample data points into existing embeddings, and the tight boundary of alpha hulls might leave some points outside of the ecompassed region.

Using kernel density estimates to extract regions can also miss smaller clusters of data points. Due to the nature of kernel density estimation, and our choice to extract regions at a prespecified contour level, some small clusters could easily be missed, even if they are compeltely enriched with a single variable value, simply because there are not enough data points in the cluster. Developing alternative techniques for region estimation is a good avenue for future work.

Our approach achieves scalability in the number of data points through sampling. By default, we limit the number of data points to 5,000 and use those to estimate regions and contruct visual explanations. Our approach works quite well even when this number is increased to e.g. 10,000, but the results are largely the same. Our approach is generally not limited by the number of data points, but rather, the number of different variables. For each variable, we perform several computatiation intesive tasks. We first apply K-Means discretization to each variable, then perform kernel density estimates for each variable. We then determine the number of sample data points that fall within each region . Finally, each region undergoes an iterative post-hoc iterative merging process which primarily depends on the number of discretization bins or the number of different categories in the categorical variables. Data sets containing thousands to tens of thousands have become widespread in fields such as bioinformatics, where non-linear dimensionality reduciton methods are routinely used to inspect transcriptomics data. Developing more scalable methods capable of generating visual explanations for data sets containing thousands of variables would be of great interest.

While the focus of our VECA is to create a fully-automated pipeline for generating visual explanations of embeddings, one can easily envision an interactive system where these kinds of visual explanations could be explored in more detail by the user. For instance, a user presented with a descriptive explanation such as the one in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.e may discover the blue region corresponds to red wine, low levels of sulfur dioxide, and certain levels of chlorides. However, they may then wonder where in the plot high levels sulfur dioxide appear, in which case the user would be presented with a contrastive explanation of this variable as in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_explanations}.c. The user may similarily wonder where exactly the three regions contained within the blue region occur, and may wish to inspect the subregions, as seen in Figs.~\ref{fig:covertype_model}.c-f. Alternatively, the user may also be intersted in the purity of the region, and may wish to inspect a plot that highlights exactly which data samples exhibit the particular explanatory variable, and which such samples are missing from the regions, similar to what is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wine_separation}. Such a user interface could seamlessly be incorporated into existing visual analytics tools such as Orange~\cite{Demsar2013}.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents VECA, a visual explanation framework designed to automatically generate visual explanations for two-dimensional embeddings. Explanations are generated in terms of any variables of choice, most commonly, the input variables used to generate the embedding. VECA is able to generate two types of visual explanations: contrastive explanations, which aim to find the most informative input variables, and descriptive variables, which aim to describe as much of the space as possible using different variables or combinations of variables.
Our approach involves a novel discretization scheme based on the spatial proximities between different discretization bins in the embedding. VECA generates multiple possible visual explanations. In contrastive explanations, we develop a novel visual explanation ranking scheme, preferring layouts with little overlap that are as informative as possible. For descriptive explanations, we frame the explanation construction process as a graph-independent set problem and develop a novel ranking scheme that prefers diverse and informative visual explanations.
We demonstrate the utility of our approach on three data sets of increasing complexity. We also demonstrate how VECA can be used to gain an understanding into deep networks, uncover the types of errors these methods are likely to make, and what kinds of relationships they are likely to have learned.

We also release our reference Python implementation of VECA. VECA is available on GitHub and can be installed via PyPI. VECA is easy to use, and we have worked hard to make the parameters easy to understand. 

Overall, our work contributes to the growing field of interpretable machine learning and offers a new perspective on how to explain non-linear embeddings in terms of their original features. It also provides a new exploratory method for describing the behavior of deep neural network models.

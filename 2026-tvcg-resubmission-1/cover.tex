VERA: Generating Visual Explanations of Two-Dimensional Embeddings \\ via Region Annotation}

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent {\bf COVER LETTER}

\vspace{2mm}


\noindent Dear Editor,\\
 
\vspace{3mm}

\noindent We would like to resubmit a revised version of our manuscript ``\textit{VERA: Generating Visual Explanations of Two-Dimensional Embeddings via Region Annotation}'' to be considered for publication in the ``\textit{IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics}'' as a Regular Paper. The previously assigned log number for this manuscript is TVCG-2025-10-1088.

We thank the reviewers for the comprehensive feedback that helped us improve the manuscript and for the opportunity to submit a revised version. In response, we have substantially revised the manuscript to clarify VERA's contribution relative to prior embedding-explanation methods, strengthen the justification of key methodological choices, clarify how explanations should be interpreted, and more explicitly discuss scope, scalability, and limitations. We have also expanded the user-study description and added extensive supplementary and replication materials at \url{https://github.com/pavlin-policar/vera-paper}, including the study materials, parameter sensitivity analyses, supplementary workflows, and additional region-construction examples. In addition, because VERA's region-construction step is modular, we have updated the implementation to support the alternative rangeset-based region construction highlighted by Reviewer 1 and include supplementary examples based on this alternative.

Below, we provide detailed, point-by-point responses to the reviewers' comments. To facilitate the review process, we also include a version of the manuscript with the changes highlighted relative to the previous revision.

\subsection*{Reviewer 1}

\reviewc{r1q1}{Abstract and Introduction do not clearly convey the uniqueness of this work.
The current Abstract and Introduction only provide a general purpose of VERA. As described in Related Work, as well as one non-cited but similar work by Sohns et al., ``Attribute-based Explanation of Non-Linear Embeddings of High-Dimensional Data,'' TVCG 2022, various methods have been proposed to address visual explanations, generation of informative regions, etc. From the current Abstract and Introduction, I was not able to understand what is the uniqueness of VERA and what kind of important problems VERA tries to address. I recommend the authors to explain VERA's essential differences from other methods in Abstract and Introduction.}

\answer{%
Thank you for this suggestion. We have revised the abstract and now explicitly state that VERA's primary goal is to explain two-dimensional embeddings and, to clearly distinguish it from prior approaches, highlight that it automatically generates, filters, merges, and ranks region-based explanations, rather than merely providing visual encodings or relying on interactive exploration.

We thank the reviewer for pointing out \citet{Sohns2022}, which is indeed related to our work and should have been cited. We have added this citation and discuss the relationship between VERA and rangesets in detail in our response to the following comment.
}


\reviewc{r1q2a}{Comparison with existing visual explanation methods is limited.
First of all, I think Sohns et al.'s work (``Attribute-based Explanation of Non-Linear Embeddings of High-Dimensional Data,'' TVCG 2022) is the most closely related work. However, the authors did not cite this paper. Sohns et al.'s work has various similairties: not relying on clustering, trying to mitigate overlapping issues, applying binning to continuous attributes, etc. But, I think still VERA has a clear strength in incorporating multiple attributes in a single visualization.}

\answer{%
As the reviewer noted, rangesets are closely related to VERA. We have now added this citation and added a paragraph to the Related Work section (Section \ref{sec:related_work}) to clarify the relationship between the two approaches more explicitly.

Briefly, \citet{Sohns2022} propose a novel visual encoding -- rangesets -- for visualizing the spatial distribution of feature values in a given embedding. As the reviewer points out, their approach shares many of the advantages of our KDE approach.
However, we emphasize that defining regions is only the first stage of our system, and the novelty of VERA primarily lies in subsequent steps, i.e., filtering, merging, and ranking.
The region construction procedure is modular, and our KDE approach could be substituted by other approaches, including rangesets.

To illustrate this, we have now incorporated rangesets into our open-source implementation of VERA and now provide several examples of VERA explanations based on rangeset regions in the supplementary material.
In Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}, we additionally write: ``We note, however, that the region construction step is modular: KDE contours can be replaced by alternative region representations while leaving the remaining steps unchanged, such as the alpha hulls used in rangesets~\citep{Sohns2022}. We include several visualizations based on this variant in the supplementary material.''

We believe these changes help delineate our contributions from existing work, better demonstrate the novelty of the approach, and provide additional examples of VERA explanations.
}


\reviewc{r1q2b}{%
The user study compares VERA only with Orange, a general-purpose interactive tool. While useful, this does not demonstrate improvement over methods explicitly designed for explaining embeddings, including Sohns et al. and the attribute-based family (e.g., using multiple visualizations made with Sohns et al.'s method vs a single visualization made with VERA). Even a small-scale qualitative or quantitative comparison would strengthen the claims.}

\answer{%
This concern is closely related to Comment \ref{r2q7} raised by Reviewer~2; please see our response there for the rationale behind using Orange as a representative interactive baseline.

In the specific case of \citep{Sohns2022}, we believe the comparison would answer a somewhat different question from the one targeted in our study. As discussed in our revised Related Work section, approaches such as rangesets primarily provide visual encodings that show where feature values occur in the embedding, but they do not themselves perform the ranking and selection of explanations. A direct comparison between VERA and a full list of attribute-wise rangeset visualizations would therefore mainly test whether our explanation-selection and ranking pipeline is preferable to showing all candidate feature views and leaving the selection to the user. While this is an interesting question, it is not the main scope of the present user study.
}


\reviewc{r1q3}{VERA seems to have limited usefuleness for high-dimensional data.
I would like to see visualization results and discussions on how VERA is useful when analyzing very high-dimensional data (e.g., over 1000 features). For example, is it possible to provide practical guidance on scalability limits for practitioners? While Introduction motivates the importance of automatic generation of explanations of embeddings with cases analyzing “tens of thousands of features”, the authors did not show anything close to this scale. For example, the usage scenarios use datasets with only 33 or fewer features. This number has a strong gap from the original motivation of this work. The authors should more clarify the scope of this work.}

\answer{%
Thank you for pointing this out. We agree, and have modified the writing to stress that VERA is best suited for datasets with up to several hundred features, where meaningful explanations can be expressed in terms of a relatively small subset of variables. We have made this explicit in the first paragraph in the Introduction, stressed the limitation to work with high-dimensional data in the section on Scalability and Limitations (see the writing on Visual Limitations), and added to Conclusion the need to ``improving scalability to very large feature sets through feature aggregation or abstraction'' in the further work.
}


\reviewc{r1q4}{Several explanations and reasoning for the algorithm are missing.
The authors introduced several settings/designs for VERA, but the reasoning for many of them is not explained, and the design choices remain looking arbitrary.

For example, why ``$0.25 \times \text{MaxD}$'', ``$k=\sqrt N$'' are reasonable default parameters? How should we set “a predetermined threshold” for the region merging. What can be considered as “nearly perfect” overlapping? When |P| > 3, what will happen for the human annotation score? Why aggregating multiple scores with a weighted mean rank is a good idea?}

\answer{%
We agree that the previous version of our manuscript did not sufficiently justify several of the design choices and default parameters used in VERA.

For each parameter, we include a short justification, writing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}: ``We extract contour lines of the KDE at a user-specified level, defaulting to $0.25 \times \max D$, and use the resulting polygons as the visual representation of a region $r_i$.
    This value provides a practical middle ground: lower levels produce broader, less localized regions, whereas higher levels restrict contours to dense cores and can fragment them.''
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}: ``By default, we set $\sigma$ to equal the median distance of each point's $k$-th nearest neighbor, setting $k=\sqrt{N}$. This yields a scale-adaptive local bandwidth that grows with the sample size while remaining sensitive to local structure.''
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:post-hoc-merge}: ``A pair of regions is merged when their maximum overlap exceeds a user-specified threshold. We use a conservative default so that clearly redundant regions are consolidated while nearby but distinct structures remain separate.''
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:contrastive-selection}: ``Because no single metric reliably produced good rankings across datasets, we aggregate them using a weighted mean-rank strategy. This treats the criteria as complementary heuristics and avoids relying on any one metric alone.''
\end{enumerate}

We also include a dedicated parameter sensitivity analysis in the supplementary material, together with practical guidance on parameter selection; see Comment~\ref{r2q1}.
}


\reviewc{r1q5}{While VERA merges multiple attribute values for explanations, what are the strengths of this when compared with looking multiple visulizations, each of which corresponding to an important attribute, e.g., Fig. 6a vs Fig. 6d (note: this is not evaluated through a user study)?}

\answer{%
We agree that this specific comparison was not isolated in the user study. The main advantage of the contrastive merge over showing multiple separate feature visualizations is that it avoids the need for users to mentally align nearly identical spatial patterns across panels, instead summarizing similar features in a single explanation and making both redundancy and feature relationships explicit. To clarify this, we have revised Section~\ref{sec:contrastive} to explicitly state this benefit and updated the caption of Fig.~\ref{fig:contrastive_merge} to note that the merge avoids manual comparison of nearly identical visualizations.
}


\reviewc{r1q6}{For example, based on Fig. 8, VERA tends to generate very long descriptions. Although the authors discussed LLMs might be able to make them more concise, as these descriptions are simply a list of attribute ranges/values, I think it is difficult to shorten then even with LLMs.}

\answer{We maintain that LLMs offer a promising avenue for generating short, informative descriptions. Several recent publications show that they can be used to generate useful cluster annotations, e.g., topics from keywords in text data~\citep{Kozlowski2024,Piper2025} as well as textual descriptions of clusters or general, tabular data~\citep{Preiss2024,Hong2025,Buchmuller2026}. It would be fairly straightforward to adapt and incorporate any of these methods into our existing pipeline, or to build on this recent research and tailor cluster names to the domain at hand. However, a procedure like this would need to be carefully designed and evaluated, therefore, we leave this to future work.}


\reviewc{r1q7}{Also, even with VERA, it looks like we need to see multiple visualizations to understand embeddings (e.g., Fig. 8a–d).}

\answer{%
We agree that embeddings of most real-world data often require multiple complementary views. This is precisely why VERA is designed as a ranked small-multiple summary rather than as a single all-in-one visualization: attempting to show all spatial differences in one panel would produce overlapping regions and labels and quickly become unreadable.

The key benefit of VERA is therefore not that it always reduces the explanation to a single plot, but that it automatically filters uninformative features and selects a small number of informative panels. In Fig.~\ref{fig:attrition}, for example, VERA highlights four of the highest-ranked panels derived from the 33 available features. Without this selection step, the user would instead need to manually inspect feature distributions across all available variables.
}

\subsection*{Reviewer 2}

\reviewc{r2q1}{To detect the regions, a larger number of processing steps needs to be executed, each of which introduces parameters. The choice of these parameters can severely affect the outcome. The influence of the parameters is not discussed in the paper. The list of parameters includes: the number of bins for numerical attributes, the level for the contour lines, the Gaussian kernel bandwidth, the maximum overlap threshold, the human-aware attention score, the weights for the final ranking, the threshold for near-perfect overlap, the overlap threshold for combining regions, and the number of layouts generated. The method needs to be evaluated with respect to the parameter selections.}

\answer{We agree that the original manuscript did not sufficiently discuss the effects of the method parameters or justify the default choices used throughout the paper. To address this, we now include a dedicated parameter sensitivity study on eight different data sets in the supplementary materials. The study examines the effects of the main parameters in both the region construction and explanation generation stages. The results show that the default settings used in the manuscript generally yield high-quality explanations.

We have added the following text to the end of the Methods section: ``Across the full pipeline, from region construction to explanation generation, our approach involves several user-settable parameters. In practice, the results are robust across a range of parameter settings, and the default values generally yield high-quality explanations. Further details are provided in the parameter sensitivity study in the supplementary materials.''
}


\reviewc{r2q2}{I would expect the binning of numerical attributes to be crucial. Not only does the number of bins need to be chosen, but also an appropriate binning strategy. The binning strategy is not explained in the paper. Looking at the example, it does not appear to be equidistant range binning. So, what is it? Is the binning based on percentiles? Neither of the two strategies would be perfect. I would expect that a 1D clustering would actually work best. However, that may also not be perfect for multivariate clusters. So, I really wonder what is being done here, what alternatives were considered, how the design choice was made, and how it was evaluated.}

\answer{%
We thank the reviewer for raising this point. In fact, the discretization strategy used in VERA already follows the direction suggested by the reviewer: for continuous variables, we use univariate $k$-means binning rather than equal-width or percentile-based bins.

We have now clarified this explicitly in the manuscript and added a short rationale for this choice: ``For numeric features, we discretize their values using univariate $k$-means binning, representing each bin by a binary indicator variable that denotes whether a sample falls within the corresponding interval.'' and the following footnote: ``We selected $k$-means binning because it better captures skewed and multimodal feature distributions, producing intervals that more closely follow the natural value groupings present in the data.''

While we did not perform a dedicated comparison of alternative discretization strategies in the current paper, the supplementary parameter study does evaluate the effect of the number of discretization bins across eight datasets. These results show that the default choice of five bins generally yields stable and informative explanations. Moreover, the subsequent post-hoc merging step often absorbs moderate differences introduced by finer or coarser discretization, making the pipeline reasonably robust to the choice of $k$ within this discretization scheme. We found that intervals that better follow the empirical one-dimensional structure of a feature tend to produce cleaner candidate regions, whereas coarser or less adaptive bin boundaries may split visually coherent groups or merge distinct ones prematurely, making the downstream post-hoc merging step less effective.
}


\reviewc{r2q3}{When looking at the left image of Figure 6(d), I think that the annotations that are generated by the descriptive layouts may be misleading. This annotation seems to indicate that all English books are in the lower part of the embedding, which is not true. I would assume that this would be a problem for users to correctly interpret the annotated plots. The annotations look like filters, i.e., like "shall me all English books", but they are not. I find this highly confusing.}

\answer{%
We agree that the descriptive annotation in Fig.~\ref{fig:descriptive_merge}.d can be misread as indicating where all English books are located in the embedding, rather than which feature values are characteristic of the highlighted region. Descriptive explanations are intended to characterize highlighted regions locally, whereas contrastive explanations are the appropriate view for questions about the global spatial distribution of a feature value.

To clarify this distinction, we revised the manuscript in three places:
\begin{enumerate}
\item First, we have revised the text in the second paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:methods}: ``\textit{contrastive explanations}, showing how the values of a high-dimensional feature vary across the embedding space, and \textit{descriptive explanations}, summarizing feature values characteristic of individual highlighted regions.''

\item Second, we have revised the opening paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:descriptive-explanations}: ``Descriptive explanations characterize regions of the embedding space by identifying combinations of features and feature values that are characteristic of the samples within those regions. Unlike contrastive explanations, which indicate where different feature values occur across the embedding space, descriptive explanations should be interpreted as local summaries of highlighted regions.''

\item Third, we have added to the third paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:descriptive-explanations:construction}: ``Here, English serves as an additional descriptor of the highlighted region rather than indicating where all English books appear in the embedding.''
\end{enumerate}
}


\reviewc{r2q4}{The human-attention score assumes that 3 is the perfect number of clusters to show simultaneously. Why 3? The authors reference some papers that hint at using 7, 4, or 2. I would think that it depends on the data distributions. As long as clusters are clearly separated, I would add as many as possible. For example, if a dataset consists of 9 separated clusters in the 2D embedding, then the approach would most likely generate 3 layouts with 3 clusters each, while a single layout may explain the entire data instead. That sounds suboptimal to me.}

\answer{%
We agree that there is no universally optimal number of region annotations to show in a single panel, and that this depends on the spatial structure of the embedding. Our intent was not to claim that three is always the correct number, but to introduce a soft preference for moderately sized, more readable panels. In both the contrastive and descriptive cases, the human attention score is only one component of the final ranking and therefore acts as a heuristic bias rather than as a hard constraint. Panels with more than three annotations are not excluded, and can still be selected when they score better on the other criteria, such as overlap, purity, coverage, or within-panel contrastivity.

To clarify this, we made the following revisions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:contrastive-selection}, we now write ``we therefore include a \textit{human attention score} as a heuristic readability prior'' and ``Here, three serves as a practical middle ground rather than a universal optimum.''
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:descriptive-explanations:selection}, we now write ``These metrics favor panels that offer broad coverage of the embedding space, contain reliable regions, and remain visually manageable, while softly preferring simpler panels over more crowded ones.''
\end{enumerate}
}


\reviewc{r2q5}{I also think that occlusion is a big issue for the presented method. The contours encircle regions that are filled with points colored in the respective color. These highlighted colored points occlude the non-colored ones. Sometimes there seems to be no not-colored points, but they are simply occluded.}

\answer{%
We agree that point-level occlusion is an important limitation of static scatter plot-based visualizations. However, VERA is primarily a region-based explanation method: the contours and labels are the main explanatory objects, while the scatter points provide local context. Our pipeline therefore aims to reduce region-level overlap through merging, filtering, and low-overlap panel selection.
We now clarify this in Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}, adding ``In the resulting visualizations, these contours serve as the primary representation of region annotations, while the scatter plot provides contextual sample locations.''

In addition, our implementation includes an optional purity-aware rendering that deemphasizes lower-purity regions through lighter fills and thinner boundaries. We include examples of this auxiliary rendering in the supplementary material. While this does not eliminate occlusion, it helps visually deemphasize more ambiguous regions, and we now include supplementary comparisons of the default and purity-aware renderings.

}


\reviewc{r2q6}{The user study is not sufficiently explained. I would need to see the full catalogue of questions, including the visualizations, to judge its validity. \reviewc{r2q7}{For the study, the Orange data mining toolkit was used as a baseline, which is a toolkit that was published in a machine learning journal. Honestly, I do not know this toolkit and am not aware of its functionalities. I would need an explanation of the tool. But, why not use something that is established in the visualization community? \answer{%
Our intent in the user study was not to claim superiority over a particular visualization system, but to compare VERA against a representative baseline for interpreting embeddings.
We chose Orange because many related embedding-interpretation workflows, including those based on feature distribution plots and discriminative models, can be reproduced in Orange using simple widget-based workflows. To make this clearer, we expanded Section~\ref{sec:user-study:design} and now state: ``We selected the Orange data mining toolkit as our interactive baseline because of its ease of use and support for exploratory analysis through interactive brushing and linked views.'' We also now add: ``We provide several example workflows in the supplementary materials.''

Secondly, our students were already familiar with Orange from prior coursework.
Since the study outcome includes response time, this reduced additional interface-learning overhead for the interactive baseline and helped limit confounding due to tool onboarding.

}


\reviewc{r2q8}{Combinations of semantically compatible rules are allowed, where, for categorical attributes, all combinations are compatible. However, it could be a projection artifact that two categories overlap. Is this considered somehow?}

\answer{%
Projection artifacts can indeed create apparent overlaps between categories in the 2D embedding. We do not claim to resolve this issue in all cases. Instead, when suitable sample-wise distortion measures are available for a given embedding, VERA can incorporate them as weights during KDE estimation, which downweights poorly mapped samples and makes spurious regions in distorted parts of the embedding less likely to be extracted in the first place. Since different dimensionality reduction approaches may call for different distortion measures, we leave this choice to the user rather than assuming a single universally appropriate correction.

We now clarify this in Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}: ``When sample-wise distortion measures are available, we downweight poorly mapped samples during KDE estimation, making spurious regions in distorted parts of the embedding less likely to be extracted.'' The accompanying note further explains that such regions likely correspond to spurious artifacts of the dimensionality reduction process rather than true underlying structure.
}


\reviewc{r2q9}{The descriptions become longish. For the synthetic 7D dataset, there are descriptions with 6 components. This does not scale very well to higher dimensionality.}

\answer{%
We agree that this is one of the limitations of our approach. We already discuss this limitation explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:scalability}, writing: ``long descriptor lists impose a cognitive burden on users, who must manually inspect and interpret the output. Second, these lists consume screen space, leading to visual clutter: labels may overlap with each other or with data points, extend off-screen, or otherwise obstruct the view''. We also point to a possible way of addressing this issue in the following sentence: ``Future work could address these issues by leveraging large language models (LLMs) to automatically generate concise, informative summaries of lengthy region descriptors.'' Please also refer to Comment~\ref{r1q6}, where we cite several recent publications on related LLM-based annotation and summarization approaches.
}


\reviewc{r2q10}{The rankings use the mean of a number of measures. However, maybe one of the measures is actually more suitable than others. Has this been evaluated?}

\answer{%
We did not perform a dedicated ablation to determine whether any single ranking criterion is consistently more suitable than the others, nor did we conduct a user study aimed at identifying an optimal aggregation of these criteria. Our motivation for aggregating multiple criteria was that they capture different desirable properties of an explanation, including spatial separation, region reliability, coverage, and visual manageability. As we now clarify in the manuscript, ``no single metric reliably produced good rankings across datasets, we aggregate them using a weighted mean-rank strategy.''
We therefore view the current ranking as a heuristic aggregation of complementary criteria, chosen because it empirically produced reasonable rankings across datasets, rather than as a claim that the present weighting is optimal. A more systematic comparison of single-metric rankings and alternative aggregation schemes would be a valuable direction for future work.
}


\reviewc{r2q11}{The Iris and Penguins datasets actually used the class label as a feature. Then, obviously, the three classes can be found. I think this is not how it should be done.}

\answer{%
The reviewer raises an important point. We agree that class labels should not be used to construct the embedding itself. In our examples, however, the class variable was not used in the dimensionality reduction step for any of the data sets; it was only used afterward as a post hoc explanatory variable. We believe this is appropriate because embeddings are often inspected precisely to assess class separability and to understand which regions correspond to known categories. In that setting, the class label serves as a reference variable for interpretation and debugging rather than as an input used to generate the embedding. We clarify this distinction explicitly in the opening paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:case-studies} (Usage Scenarios): ``When a dataset includes a known class or target variable, we use it only as a post hoc variable for interpreting the embedding and do not use it to construct the embedding itself.''
}



\subsection*{Reviewer 3}

\reviewc{r3q1}{The motivation for using KDE should be strengthened. Explain why it is an appropriate solution, and what advantages it offers.}

\answer{%
We agree that our motivation for using KDE could be strengthened and have now added the following to Section~\ref{sec:region_construction}: ``Using KDE contours as regions provides several advantages: most importantly, it removes the need for a separate clustering step in the 2D embedding, which allows VERA to uncover continuous transitions rather than only cluster assignments. KDEs can also be computed efficiently, scaling linearly with the number of samples, and allow distortion measures to be incorporated directly during region construction.''
%We also note that KDE-based contour overlays are widely used in scatterplot visualization as a means of depicting shared regions in point-based data and have been used extensively in prior work such as splatterplots \citep{MayorgaGleicher2013}, DataContextMap \citep{ChengMueller2016}, and related approaches \citep{Li2022,Li2023,Ye2025}.

We also clarify that region construction is modular and that KDE contours can be replaced by alternative region representations, such as the alpha hulls used in rangesets~\citep{Sohns2022}: ``We note, however, that the region construction step is modular: KDE contours can be replaced by alternative region representations while leaving the remaining steps unchanged, such as the alpha hulls used in rangesets~\cite{Sohns2022}.'' We also now provide several examples of this rangeset-based variant in the supplementary material.
}


\reviewc{r3q2}{The paper lacks proper comparison and positioning with respect to related methods. Without quantitative and qualitative comparisons, it is hard to judge whether VERA provides a substantial advance over existing methods. A more systematic discussion of their similarities, differences, and respective strengths/limitations is necessary to clarify the contribution.}

\answer{%
Thank you for this comment. To better address it, we have revised the Related Work section to more clearly distinguish between direct feature-encoding approaches (e.g., rangesets) and explanation-generation methods, and to explicitly position VERA within the latter category. In particular, we clarify that VERA extends beyond region construction by introducing automated filtering, merging, and ranking of candidate explanations, thereby reducing the need for manual inspection. We also added a discussion of the relationship to \citet{Sohns2022}, highlighting both similarities (e.g., region construction without clustering) and key differences (i.e., explanation selection and aggregation). These revisions should provide a more systematic comparison of the respective roles, strengths, and limitations of existing approaches and better clarify VERA’s contribution. Note that clarification of contributions was also requested by other reviewers, for example, please see our responses to Comments~\ref{r1q1} and \ref{r1q2a}.
}

\reviewc{r3q3}{The use cases are interesting, but they are built upon small datasets, raising doubts about the scalability of the method.}

\answer{%
Scalability is already explicitly addressed in Section~\ref{sec:scalability} (``Scalability and Limitations'') and in Fig. 8, where we report both algorithmic complexity and empirical runtime on synthetic datasets of varying size. In particular, we show that runtime scales linearly with the number of samples during region construction, while the main computational bottleneck is the number of extracted region annotations/features. We also discuss the higher complexity of descriptive explanation generation and note a greedy approximation for larger problems. We agree that the illustrative case studies themselves use relatively small to medium-sized datasets, but they are complemented by the dedicated scalability analysis.

Furthermore, this revision adds supplementary material with a detailed parameter sensitivity analysis on eight datasets, including the Forest Covertype dataset~\cite{covertype_31} with over 581,000 samples. While this large-scale example also shows that the method remains applicable well beyond the dataset sizes used in the main paper, we include it in the supplementary material because its primary purpose is to illustrate parameter sensitivity rather than to serve as a qualitative case study.
}


\reviewc{r3q4}{The method appears to be sensitive to several parameters/thresholds. The authors are encouraged to perform a more thorough analysis on them and provide concrete guidelines for threshold settings, making it more practical for end users.}

\answer{%
Agreed. We now include a dedicated parameter sensitivity analysis in the supplementary material, together with practical guidance on parameter selection; see Comment~\ref{r2q1}.
}


\reviewc{r3q5}{The user evaluation results seem to show slight improvements when compared with the Orange tool used in the study. However, the question is whether it is worth the effort to achieve minor improvements.}

\answer{%
While we agree that the user study does not show a large gain in accuracy, our central result is that VERA enables users to answer typical embedding-interpretation questions with comparable accuracy but significantly less time and effort. In the study, participants were approximately 33\% faster with VERA while maintaining similar accuracy. We consider this gain practically meaningful, since users typically inspect many embeddings and repeatedly answer similar questions, so a 33\% reduction in response time substantially reduces cumulative effort.

This interpretation is already reflected in several places in the manuscript. For instance, in the Abstract, we state that VERA requires ``significantly less time and user effort.'' In the Introduction, we write that static approaches ``can substantially reduce analysis time and cognitive effort.'' In the Results section, we already summarize the study by stating that participants answered with ``roughly the same degree of accuracy but at a much faster rate'' when using VERA explanations.

To make this point even clearer, we have now added the following text to Section~\ref{sec:user-study:results}: ``We consider this gain practically meaningful, since analysts typically inspect many embeddings and perform similar interpretation tasks; in that setting, a 33\% reduction in response time substantially reduces cumulative effort.''
}
